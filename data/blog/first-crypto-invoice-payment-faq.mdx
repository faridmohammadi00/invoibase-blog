# First Crypto Invoice Payment: A Freelancer FAQ

You already know how to invoice. Crypto settlement adds three new questions:

1. Where do the funds land?
2. Which network is safe to use?
3. What do I tell my accountant?

This FAQ is for freelancers and small agencies taking that first USDC/USDT payment without turning it into a research project.

## Do I need a wallet before I can get paid?

**Yes — you need an address you control.** That can be:

- A self-custody wallet (MetaMask, Rabby, Phantom, TronLink, etc.)
- An exchange deposit address (**higher freeze/withdrawal risk**; fine for experiments, weaker for ongoing freelance income)

Non-custodial invoicing means the **client pays your address directly**. The invoicing software should verify the transfer — it should not hold the money “for later.”

If you have never created a wallet:

1. Install one reputable wallet for the network you expect clients to use (EVM or Solana is enough to start)
2. Write down the recovery phrase offline — never in screenshots or Notion
3. Copy the **receive address** carefully
4. Send yourself a tiny test amount before sharing it on a large invoice

## Do my clients need to “learn crypto”?

Not as much as marketing tweets imply. Many clients already:

- Hold USDT on an exchange and can withdraw on TRON or an EVM network
- Use MetaMask / Coinbase Wallet for USDC on Polygon or Base
- Use Phantom for Solana USDC

Your job is to make the pay page explicit: **exact token + exact network + exact amount**. Confusion — not cryptography — is the usual blocker.

## What should my first invoice ask for?

Start simple:

- Invoice currency: **USD** (so the scope of work stays familiar)
- Settlement: **USDC** on **Polygon** *or* **USDT** on **TRON**, depending on the client
- Due date and line items as usual

Avoid starting with volatile assets (ETH/SOL) unless the client specifically wants that. Stablecoins keep the “$2,000 invoice” story intact.

## What does “non-custodial” mean for me?

| Custodial processor | Non-custodial invoice |
| --- | --- |
| Client pays a platform | Client pays your wallet |
| Platform may delay payout | Funds arrive when the chain confirms |
| Extra counterparty risk | You control the private keys / exchange account |

Non-custodial does **not** mean “no paperwork.” You still issue a professional invoice and keep records. It means the settlement hop does not park funds on the invoicing company’s balance sheet.

## How do I know the payment actually arrived?

Prefer a workflow that:

1. Shows a public pay page with the amount and network
2. Detects the transaction on-chain
3. Marks the invoice paid and stores the **transaction hash**
4. Emails a receipt you can forward to accounting

If you are verifying manually, open a block explorer for that network, paste the tx hash, and confirm:

- Correct token contract  
- Correct destination address  
- Amount within your tolerance  
- Enough confirmations for your comfort level  

## What about taxes?

Rules vary by country. Common freelancer practice:

- Treat the **USD invoice amount** as the economic value of the work
- Keep the **tx hash**, timestamp, token, network, and client name
- Note the fair-market USD value at receipt if your jurisdiction requires crypto-asset tracking
- Ask a tax professional before year-end — especially if you convert to local fiat later

Do not assume “crypto payment = untaxed income.”

## What if the client sends on the wrong network?

This is the #1 failure mode.

Prevention beats recovery:

- Put network warnings on the pay page (“Send only USDC on Polygon”)
- Confirm the client’s withdrawal screen before they submit large amounts
- For first-time clients, request a tiny test transfer

Recovery depends on the chain and whether funds are recoverable at all. Sometimes they are not. Treat wrong-network sends as preventable outages, not support tickets you can always reverse.

## How do recurring clients work?

Once a client successfully pays on a rail, **reuse that default**:

- Same token  
- Same network  
- Same wallet address (unless you rotate for security)

Consistency reduces support load more than optimizing another $0.02 of gas.

## Minimal starter stack (recommended)

1. One EVM wallet (Polygon/Base USDC)
2. Optional: TronLink or exchange TRC-20 address if your clients are USDT-heavy
3. Optional: Phantom if you work with Solana natives
4. An invoicing tool that issues USD invoices and verifies on-chain settlement
5. A folder (or accounting tool) for PDF invoice + receipt + tx hash

## Quick answers

**Is crypto invoicing only for Web3 agencies?**  
No. Any freelancer with international clients can use stablecoins as a settlement rail while keeping the invoice in USD.

**Should I put my seed phrase in the invoice software?**  
Never. Share only public addresses.

**Is USDT or USDC “safer”?**  
They are different issuer and market-risk profiles. Pick the asset your client can send reliably, then document it. For U.S.-facing regulatory context, see our [GENIUS Act overview](/blog/genius-act-usdc-usdt-freelancer-payments).

**What if I only want fiat at the end?**  
Receive stablecoins, then convert through an exchange or on-ramp/off-ramp you trust. The invoice layer and the conversion layer can be separate decisions.

## Bottom line

Your first crypto invoice payment does not require becoming a trader. It requires:

- A wallet address you control  
- A clear token + network pair  
- A verification trail your future self (and accountant) can read  

Start with one stablecoin rail, win one successful payment, then expand.
